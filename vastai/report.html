Two RTX PRO 6000 against the DGX Spark pair, serving coding agents

One 88-minute session on Vast.ai answered the question we could not answer on paper: can a pair of RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell cards serve Qwen3.8-Flash-Next to a team of engineers running coding agents, and how many of them, compared with the two DGX Sparks we run today.

What was compared

Same model family, same load generator, same 65k-token agentic workload. The checkpoints differ: the Spark pair serves a custom hybrid quantisation through the Marlin W4A16 kernel because GB10 has no usable FP4 path in vLLM; the RTX box serves NVIDIA's stock NVFP4 checkpoint through FlashInfer's CUTLASS W4A4 kernel, which vLLM picked automatically for SM120.

Per-user decode speed as users pile on

The number an engineer feels: tokens per second on their own stream while others share the box. Bands mark what the Spark pair delivers at two and three users today, which is the bar for "good enough".

Time to first token

p50 is the typical turn, p95 the one an engineer notices. On the RTX box TTFT stays near one second up to 24 sessions and the tail is what degrades; the Spark pair's tail is worse at two users than the RTX box at 24.

Total throughput and the capacity ceiling

Reading the ceiling. Holding the experience engineers get from Spark today at two users, 45 tok/s and a five-second p95, the RTX pair carries 24 sessions. At the three-user Spark experience, 35 tok/s, it carries 32. Beyond 32 the box is compute-bound: aggregate stops growing, queueing appears at 48, and per-session decode drops to 25 tok/s. Memory is not the limit at any of these levels: KV usage peaked at 27%.

Tuning arms

Five configurations were measured on the box at 12, 24 and 32 sessions. Speculative decoding earns its keep below saturation and stops mattering at it; a larger prefill budget does the opposite.

Restart cost per configuration change

Kill-to-ready for each successful boot. Weight load is 50–65 s; the rest is torch.compile and CUDA-graph capture. The Spark pair's production boot is about 12 minutes, so an A/B that costs one afternoon there costs one coffee here.

The Spark pair's real advantage is power: it idles at 10 W per box and peaks near 55 W. The RTX pair drew up to 529 W per card under load. For a small team the Spark's per-user numbers are workable at two or three sessions; the RTX pair is the answer when the question is "how many engineers".

Method and caveats

  • Workload. bench/loadgen.py --workload agentic: one turn of a multi-turn tool-calling coding session, ~65k-token prompt, ~2–4k uncached tokens per turn, natural stops, server-default sampling (temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95). Three sequential turns per session, one rep per level. The Spark agentic reference is the same harness with three reps.
  • Checkpoint difference. Spark serves a custom hybrid quantisation; the RTX box serves NVIDIA's stock NVFP4. Acceptance rates match to within a point, which argues the models behave alike, but this is a platform comparison, not a kernel-for-kernel one.
  • Client bug, found and fixed. The load generator's thread pool silently capped in-flight requests at min(32, cores+4): 18 from the laptop, 32 on the box. Levels above the cap were rerun after the fix; the plotted RTX curve uses only valid runs. Spark runs never exceeded c=12, below the cap.
  • Warm cache. All plotted levels ran with the shared 60k prefix cached, the same 94% hit rate production sees. Cold-start behaviour was measured once: eight simultaneous cold 64k prompts gave a 32 s p95 TTFT on the RTX box.
  • Single rep. RTX levels are one rep each; Spark's rep-to-rep spread on this workload is ~7%. Differences under 10% between RTX arms should be read as ties.
  • Provenance. Image vllm/vllm-openai:nightly-d05da62e, driver 595.84, every serve line, boot log, GPU sample and raw loadgen JSON is in the repository under vastai/.
